Overview
In the DIY Serpentine Optical Interferometer using a Savart-type birefringent beam splitter and a closed-loop serpentine beam path, we observed characteristic four-color interference patterns during the diffraction-to-interference transition. These colors appear as Red–Green (top row) and Blue–Yellow (bottom row) when the system is observed through a polarizing film.
This document summarizes the optical significance of these four colors and their deep conceptual connection to modern condensed matter physics, particularly moiré patterns, flat bands, and phonon-exciton-polaritons in graphene heterostructures.
The Four Interference Colors (RGYB)

Top-left: Red
Top-right: Green
Bottom-left: Blue
Bottom-right: Yellow

These four colors are not arbitrary. They represent the most prominent interference colors arising from the phase retardation (birefringence) between the ordinary and extraordinary rays in the Savart plate + polarizing film system.
As the Z-axis is rotated, the optical path difference changes, producing constructive and destructive interference that varies with wavelength. This results in vivid complementary color pairs (Red ↔ Cyan, Green ↔ Magenta, Blue ↔ Yellow) that visually demonstrate wave superposition and the transition from particle-like spots to wave-like fringe patterns.
Physical Interpretation: Wave-Particle Duality
The appearance of extended colored fringes and Moiré-like structures clearly illustrates the wave nature of light. The central bright spot behaves particle-like (localized), while the surrounding fringes and satellite spots reveal the extended wave character — a macroscopic demonstration of wave-particle duality in an optical interferometer.
Connection to Graphene Heterostructures and Moiré Physics
The optical interference observed in this setup serves as a macroscopic analog of phenomena occurring at the atomic scale in twisted graphene and transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) heterostructures:

Moiré Pattern: Just as two slightly mismatched or rotated lattices create a long-wavelength moiré superlattice, the interference between two orthogonally polarized beams in the serpentine interferometer produces periodic fringe/Moiré structures.
Flat Bands: In moiré systems, interference-induced periodic potentials lead to flattened electronic bands, enabling strong electron correlations. Similarly, specific phase retardations in the interferometer produce stable, high-contrast interference nodes.
Phonon-Exciton-Polaritons: In graphene heterostructures, photons, excitons, and phonons can strongly (or weakly) couple to form hybrid quasiparticles. The color-dependent interference in our system reflects wavelength-selective coupling, analogous to polariton formation.
Strong vs Weak Coupling: The transition between satellite spots + fringes (strong interference regime) and their suppression by UV or aperture edge effects mirrors the tuning of coupling strength in moiré systems via twist angle, strain, or dielectric environment.

The closed-loop serpentine geometry further suggests possible topological phase effects (e.g., Möbius-type Berry phase locking), which are also actively studied in twisted bilayer graphene and photonic topological insulators.
Experimental Context
These four-color patterns are most clearly observed under Condition A (polarizing film only). They are significantly suppressed under Condition B (black paper aperture) and almost completely eliminated under Condition C (UV irradiation), leaving only the central interference node. This tunability demonstrates optical analogs of gating and environmental control in 2D material systems.
Significance
This low-cost DIY interferometer provides an accessible optical platform to visualize and explore concepts that are otherwise only accessible through expensive nanofabrication and cryogenic setups in condensed matter physics. It bridges classical optics with modern twistronics and polaritonics.
Although four characteristic interference colors (RGYB) are theoretically expected in birefringent systems with white light, the current red diode laser produces primarily monochromatic red patterns on the screen.
